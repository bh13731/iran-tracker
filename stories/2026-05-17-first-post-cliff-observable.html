The First Post-Cliff Observable: How May 21–24 Will Empirically Test Which Redistribution Rule the World Is Consistent With

May 17, 2026 — Day 40 Analysis Cease-fire Hormuz
Day 40 of cease-fire. P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, static for 19 consecutive days. Three days remain to the May 20 partial-arrangement cliff. The May 14 story published the redistribution problem and the May 15 methodology update pre-committed smoothed Rule C as the primary redistribution choice. That pre-commitment is now empirically testable. Rules A (proportional), B (all-into-stalemate), and C (asymmetric / rupture-weighted) produce identifiably different posteriors over what the 72–96 hours immediately after the cliff fires should look like. A fresh naval-incident cluster corroborates Rule C. A near-zero-event 72 hours corroborates Rule B. A neutral Gulf MoD or ISW posture revision corroborates Rule A. May 21–24 is therefore a natural experiment against the rule choice — the only window in which the three rules diverge sharply in their predictions before reconverging around ~May 28 as the watch-list base rates dominate. The cliff fires May 20; the first methodology test fires May 21–24.

What the Three Rules Actually Predict

The May 14 story decomposed the redistribution problem analytically. Each rule answers the same question — where does the partial-arrangement scenario’s 15 pp of scenario probability redistribute when its P(open | scenario) value drops to zero — but they answer it via structurally different mechanisms, and each mechanism implies a different posterior over the immediate post-cliff signal stream.

Rule B says the world should be empty for 96 hours. Rule A says the world should produce a routine anchor update. Rule C says the world should produce a fresh negative-side event. These are visually distinguishable outcomes — not subtly different posterior densities, but genuinely separable observational regimes.

Why the Test Window Closes Quickly

The natural-experiment window is narrow because the three rules’ marginal predictions reconverge after the first 7–10 days. The mechanism is straightforward: each rule has a different posterior over the immediate aftermath of the eviction, but all three rules then converge on the same long-run watch-list base rates derived from the surviving three scenarios. By ~May 28, the differences in posterior signal density become small enough that observed events are consistent with all three rules with similar likelihood.

Concretely, the base-rate trigger probabilities for the four surviving watch-list indicators are roughly:

The Rule C uplift on the naval-incident channel is the discriminating signal — not the absolute probability, but the asymmetry across rules.

The Three Discriminating Signals

Translating the posterior expectations into observable signal classes, the May 21–24 window has three distinct outcome regimes that map cleanly onto the three rules:

Signal class 1 — Naval-escalation cluster (corroborates Rule C)

A fresh IRGC naval incident within 72 hours of the cliff — whether a third commercial vessel seizure, an explicit escalatory statement tied to the partial-arrangement closure, a sailor or vessel released as a demand-articulation signal, or a probing maneuver against a Gulf-state-flagged ship — would corroborate the Rule C mechanism. The logic: if closing the visible diplomatic path mechanically raises the marginal attractiveness of escalation as a stalemate-breaking move, then Iran’s lowest-cost escalation channel (the IRGC Navy, which has been silent for 25 days but is the least-degraded instrument) should activate first. A Rule C-consistent outcome is one that proves the silence of the IRGC Navy since Apr 22 was contingent on the partial-arrangement scenario’s pendency, not on an underlying decision to refrain from escalation.

Signal class 2 — Continued silence (corroborates Rule B)

If the May 21–24 window produces literally nothing — no naval incidents, no anchor data, no fresh diplomatic motion, no public Iranian or US statement of strategic significance — that empty stretch is most consistent with Rule B. The logic: if the evicted scenario’s 15 pp of probability collapses entirely into the stalemate equilibrium, then the equilibrium has thickened; the world’s stable state is now even stickier than the pre-cliff 55%. A Rule B-consistent outcome is one in which the post-cliff steady state already looks indistinguishable from the pre-cliff steady state, just at a lower P(reopen) anchor.

Signal class 3 — Neutral anchor revision (corroborates Rule A)

If the window produces a routine authoritative data update — an overdue Gulf MoD cumulative tally (UAE last reported Apr 9, so a fresh update is overdue by ~42 days at the cliff date), an ISW posture revision (last special report Apr 19, so ~32 days overdue), an FDD/LWJ theater revision — that update should be substantively neutral on the directional posture: numbers revised marginally, no strong directional read. The logic: if the eviction redistributes proportionally with no mechanism privileging any particular event class, then the world produces what the world was already going to produce on its existing reporting cadence. A Rule A-consistent outcome is the most "boring" of the three — an anchor data update that arrives and lands without changing the qualitative read.

The Pre-Commitment Question

The May 15 methodology update pre-committed smoothed Rule C as the primary post-cliff redistribution rule. That pre-commitment was made because, on the evidence available May 15, Rule C’s mechanism (visible-diplomatic-closure raising marginal escalation attractiveness) is the most theoretically defensible across the three. The choice was disciplined — published before the cliff, with a smoothed weighting to avoid over-pricing the asymmetric mechanism, and with the explicit reservation that Rules A and B are also tracked.

The natural-experiment framing now lets the methodology test that pre-commitment empirically. If May 21–24 produces signal class 1, the pre-commitment is corroborated and Rule C carries forward as the primary read. If May 21–24 produces signal class 2 or class 3, the methodology has explicit evidence the pre-commitment was wrong and should revise downward to Rule B or Rule A respectively.

The asymmetric stakes are worth noting. Headline P(reopen) only spreads ~2.7 pp across the three rules, but P(rupture-scenario) spreads ~15 pp (23% under Rule B vs ~30% under Rule C, vs ~27% under Rule A). The rule choice does not move the dashboard headline much; it moves the underlying rupture-risk read substantially. A Rule C world is a meaningfully more dangerous world than a Rule B world, and the May 21–24 observable is the cleanest empirical test of which world the methodology is actually in.

What this means for May 17–20 (pre-cliff): The next 72 hours are operationally near-deterministic. The probability the cliff is averted by a Round 3 announcement passing the five-criterion credibility filter is ~2–3% over 3 days (down from the 5-day ~5% the May 15 story priced). The probability the cliff fires on schedule is therefore ~97–98%. The next analytically significant event is not the cliff itself but the methodology test that begins on May 21. The current dashboard reads should be treated as a final pre-cliff snapshot.

What the Pre-Cliff Stasis Has Told Us So Far

It is worth noting that the 19-day pre-cliff stasis is itself informative against the three rules — not directly, because the stasis predates the cliff, but indirectly, because it constrains what the three rules can plausibly predict for the post-cliff window.

Under Rule B, the post-cliff equilibrium thickens, and the natural prediction is that 19 days of silence will simply continue. The base rate of nothing-happens is the explanatory mechanism.

Under Rule C, the silence is conditional on the partial-arrangement scenario’s pendency — specifically, on Iran’s judgment that escalation now would close a diplomatic option that still has nominal probability. Once the cliff fires and that option closes, the conditional silence loses its rationale and the prediction is that escalation arrives within days.

Under Rule A, the silence is endogenous to the existing scenario probabilities, and the post-cliff prediction is whatever the pre-cliff dynamics already implied scaled up by the proportionality factor. A neutral anchor update is consistent with continuing pre-cliff dynamics under a marginally tighter probability distribution.

The 19 days of stasis do not by themselves distinguish among the rules — they are consistent with all three under their respective mechanisms. But they sharpen the predictions for the post-cliff window: under Rule C, the silence-to-escalation transition should be visible within ~72 hours; under Rule B, the silence should continue indistinguishably; under Rule A, the anchor-update cadence should resume on its usual ~30–40 day periodicity.

What the Depletion Tracker Implies for Each Rule

The frozen depletion tracker (Tracker A) carries information that is conditional on rupture — if the ceasefire holds, the depletion numbers are paused observations; if the ceasefire ruptures, they become live again. The asymmetric rule choices have different implications for how to read Tracker A’s frozen state going forward.

Under Rule C, P(rupture-scenario) post-cliff is ~30%. The reconstituted Iranian launcher count (~200, up from ~102 at the ceasefire) and the underlying ~70% BM stockpile retained means a Rule C-corroborated post-cliff world is one in which the depletion tracker should be treated as analytically near-live again, even if formally still paused, because the conditional probability of unpausing in the rolling window is meaningful.

Under Rule B, P(rupture-scenario) post-cliff is unchanged at 23%. The depletion tracker remains frozen analytically as well as formally; the Apr 19 ISW baseline is still the authoritative read and the resume-fire readiness is a static reference point.

Under Rule A, P(rupture-scenario) sits between at ~27%. The depletion tracker is somewhere between frozen and conditionally live — the resume-fire readiness should be treated as a tail-risk input but not as an active probability flow.

The May 21–24 test therefore determines not only the post-cliff headline read but also how seriously the frozen depletion tracker should be treated going forward. A Rule C corroboration means Tracker A’s reconstitution data — ~200 launchers, ~70% BMs — becomes operationally relevant to current decisions; a Rule B corroboration means it remains a paused reference. This is the second-order consequence of getting the rule choice right.

The Calendar After May 24

If the May 21–24 window produces a discriminating signal and the methodology updates its rule accordingly, the post-cliff calendar through the rolling window’s close on June 16 contains roughly 23 additional days of observation. The watch-list base rates over that span imply a cumulative ~25–35% probability of some watch-list firing — predominantly negative-side under any rule. Without a fresh authoritative anchor (overdue ISW report or Gulf MoD cumulative), the methodology will be running on stale base-rate inputs into late June.

The implication is that the May 21–24 test is not just a methodological choice but a forcing function on the cadence of authoritative reporting. The longer the ISW Apr 19 / UAE Apr 9 anchor data ages without revision, the more the methodology depends on derived inputs (watch-list firing rates) over directly observed posture data. The Rule A signal class — a routine anchor revision — would resolve that secondary problem; Rule B and Rule C signals do not.

Bottom Line

P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, unchanged for 19 consecutive days. Three days remain to the May 20 partial-arrangement cliff. The cliff is structurally near-certain (~97–98%) to fire on schedule. The methodology has pre-committed smoothed Rule C as the primary post-cliff redistribution rule, but the May 21–24 window is the only interval in which Rules A, B, and C produce identifiably different posteriors over the immediate post-cliff signal stream.

The three rules predict three distinct outcome regimes: Rule C predicts a fresh naval-escalation cluster within 72 hours; Rule B predicts continued silence; Rule A predicts a routine authoritative anchor update. By ~May 28 the rules’ marginal predictions reconverge and the natural-experiment window closes. The cliff fires May 20; the first methodology test fires May 21–24; and the outcome of that test determines both the post-cliff P(rupture-scenario) read (which spreads ~15 pp across the rules, far wider than the ~2.7 pp headline spread) and the operational treatment of the frozen depletion tracker.

The 19-day pre-cliff stasis has been substantively uninformative for the headline P(reopen) but increasingly informative for the methodology’s structural choices — first on the redistribution rule itself, then on the credibility filter, then on the watch-list phase transition, and now on the natural-experiment framing of the first post-cliff observable. The probability dashboard has been static; the methodology has been building the pre-commitments needed to read the next two weeks correctly.
