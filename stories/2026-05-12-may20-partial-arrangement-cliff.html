The May 20 Cliff: What Happens to P(Reopen) When the Partial Arrangement Scenario’s Calendar Runs Out

May 12, 2026 — Day 35 Analysis Cease-fire Hormuz
Day 35 of cease-fire. P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, static for 14 consecutive days. Eight days remain to a methodology-defined inflection point that has been visible since early May: the partial Hormuz traffic arrangement scenario contributes 9 of the 15 headline percentage points to P(reopen), but it requires a Round 3 announcement plus a roughly two-to-three-week negotiation-and-implementation cycle to execute. Beyond May 20, even an instantaneous Round 3 announcement cannot deliver a partial arrangement inside the rolling 30-day window. The cliff that has been ten days out for ten days is now eight days out, and no signal has emerged to forestall it. This is the rare update in which the most likely next move on the dial is not driven by an external event — it is a pre-committed mechanical revision the methodology has been holding off for the precise duration the calendar still allows.

The Anatomy of the 15 Percentage Points

The headline probability is a weighted sum across four exhaustive scenarios. Of the 15 pp on the board, the partial arrangement carries the largest single share — about three-fifths of it — while the comprehensive-deal tail carries the remainder. Stalemate and rupture each contribute zero, by construction, because neither scenario reopens the strait to ~normal commercial traffic.

The partial arrangement is the load-bearing scenario for any positive read on Hormuz. Take it out, and P(reopen) is just the 6 pp the comprehensive-deal tail provides — a number the tracker has not been near since the war’s opening weeks.

Why May 20 Specifically

The partial arrangement scenario requires a full mediated negotiation cycle to execute. The minimum viable path from cold start to operational implementation, anchored to the previous Round 1–2 process tempo:

  • 3–5 days: Round 3 venue and date announced; delegations confirmed; agendas pre-circulated.
  • 3–7 days: Round 3 sessions held; partial-arrangement text drafted and bracketed.
  • 5–10 days: Domestic political ratification on both sides; the partial-arrangement text becomes a published memorandum.
  • 3–7 days: Operational implementation — vessel-routing protocols stood up, IRGC stand-down orders issued and observed, first transits scheduled.

The compressed-best-case stack is roughly 13 calendar days; a realistic central case sits at 22–25. The rolling 30-day window from today closes June 11. Working backwards from June 11 with a central-case 22-day execution stack pushes the latest viable Round 3 announcement to May 20. Once that date passes without a Round 3 date in hand, the partial-arrangement scenario can no longer execute within the rolling window even if every subsequent step goes perfectly — the calendar simply runs out.

What May 21 P(Reopen) Looks Like

If May 20 passes without a Round 3 announcement, the methodology faces a forced revision. The 15% partial-arrangement scenario probability does not disappear — the scenario is still nominally on the board — but P(reopen | partial) must be recalibrated downward to reflect that the path no longer terminates inside the 30-day window. Three plausible recalibration choices, none of which requires any escalation, ceasefire move, or external signal:

Recalibration choice New P(open | partial) New partial contribution New P(reopen)
Tight: scenario evicted from window entirely 0% 0 pp ~6% (comprehensive tail only)
Central: partial arrangement still possible but mostly executes outside window 20% 3 pp ~9%
Loose: late-window quasi-arrangement possible (rapid IRGC stand-down + tacit transit) 40% 6 pp ~12%

The central case is the one the methodology is most likely to land on: the scenario remains real but its window-conditional execution probability is sharply discounted. That implies a one-day, no-event drop in P(reopen) of roughly six percentage points — from ~15% to ~9% — on May 21.

Why this is unusual: Almost every prior movement in P(reopen) has been driven by an external signal (Apr 22 seizures, Apr 22 Round 2 postponement, Apr 23 launcher reconstitution). The May 20 cliff is the opposite case — a structural decline driven entirely by calendar mechanics. The tracker’s rule that “estimate updates when new signal arrives” has an asterisk: calendar compression is treated as a passive structural factor until a threshold is crossed, at which point it becomes load-bearing.

Why the Headline Hasn’t Already Moved

A reasonable critique is that this is foreseeable and should already be priced. The methodology’s answer is no: until the calendar threshold is actually crossed, there is asymmetric tail-probability that a Round 3 date emerges in the next week. The 8-day window between today and May 20 has non-trivial probability mass on diplomatic surprise — especially given that:

  • The Apr 22 Round 2 postponement was “indefinite” in phrasing but came after a series of public statements from Pakistan and Oman expressing willingness to host.
  • Iran FM Araghchi went to Islamabad for consultations on Apr 26 (and follow-on schedules have not been disclosed). Any of those visits could produce a date.
  • The US, with three carriers in theater, has a non-trivial preference for closing the cycle before the carrier-rotation calendar forces a different conversation.
  • Chinese or Omani back-channel could announce in 24–48 hours without public preamble.

None of those is more likely than not. But cumulatively they hold open enough probability mass that pre-committing the partial-arrangement scenario to lapse today would over-react. The methodology’s discipline is to hold static until the threshold actually passes, then revise in one move. That is the design feature being stress-tested over the next eight days.

What Would Forestall the Cliff

The list of events that would prevent the May 20 mechanical revision is short, specific, and each has a defined watch-list impact:

  • A Round 3 date announced by any credible host — Pakistan, Oman, or Switzerland are the highest-probability venues; +5 to +10 pp on the dial, scenario probability shifting from stalemate back into partial arrangement.
  • A documented back-channel confirmation — US officials confirming an active US/Iran working track or Trump publicly acknowledging direct messaging; +4 to +6 pp.
  • A Chinese or Omani direct mediation push — the Beijing-non-engagement piece from May 2 identified the structural obstacles; a public reversal would carry +4 to +7 pp.
  • A unilateral IRGC ship release — the two Apr 22 seizures are reversible leverage Iran has not yet deployed (subject of the May 6 analysis); a release without quid pro quo would be a deliberate signal large enough to push partial arrangement above its current 15% floor and probably extend the calendar window via a credible “we’re negotiating” framing.

None of these are currently in evidence. The proxy escalation tripwire (a second Iraq-launched drone strike, IRGC naval seizure cluster) cuts the other way, raising rupture probability and probably pulling 2–4 pp directly off the partial-arrangement scenario by signaling Iran is not pursuing the diplomatic path. Either direction, today, the watch-list is silent.

The Cliff in the Context of the Frozen Depletion Tracker

It is worth noting that on the depletion side, this analysis would be impossible. The depletion clock is frozen at the Apr 26 data anchor — ~200 launchers, ~70% missile retention, ~40% drone retention — and the freeze is unconditional pending a ceasefire rupture. The Hormuz tracker, by contrast, has a methodology-encoded mechanism for moving the dial on time alone. That is the asymmetry the dual-tracker design was built around: depletion is a function of fire, Hormuz is a function of calendars and signals. With fire at zero for 31 days and signals at zero for 14 days, the calendar is the only remaining lever, and it is about to be pulled.

Bottom Line

P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, unchanged for 14 consecutive days. The most likely next move on the dial is not driven by escalation, rupture, or breakthrough — it is driven by the calendar. Eight days from now, on May 20, the partial-arrangement scenario’s 9 pp contribution faces mechanical eviction from the rolling 30-day window. Under the methodology’s central-case recalibration, P(reopen) drops to ~9% on May 21 with no external event required. Under the tight recalibration, it drops to ~6%, the lowest level since the war began.

The actionable read for the next eight days is narrow but specific: watch for a Round 3 date, an IRGC ship release, or a credible back-channel confirmation. Each would forestall the cliff and reset the partial-arrangement scenario. Their cumulative probability over the next eight days is real but not high. The default path is the cliff itself.
