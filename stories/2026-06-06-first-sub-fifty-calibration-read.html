A Fourth Landmark Class

Through June 5 the post-horizon regime had carried three structurally distinct classes of calendar landmark. Methodology-quality landmarks (cliff May 20, revision May 25, horizon May 28) are pre-committed dashboard transitions: rule weights, scenario probabilities, watch-list composition, or rationale-page architecture changes at the calendar trigger. Measurement-quality landmarks (UAE 50d May 29, IRGC Naval lunar-month May 30, fire-silence 50d May 31, plateau Day 40 June 2) shift either the procedural cost of a future anchor refresh or the compositional read on one of the four published scenarios without moving the dial. Pure reference-frame landmarks (June 1 month rollover, June 3 first-week-complete, June 5 Day 100 of war) are calendar transitions the regime publishes through unchanged at every architectural layer.

Today closes the set with a fourth class. The 0.9310 ≈ 48% sub-50% crossing is a calibration-quality landmark: it does not move the dial, the live ladder, the four-indicator watch list, the rolling window, or the rule; it shifts the methodology’s self-reported surprise budget. What changes at the threshold is not a published number, not a procedural layer, and not a scenario-compositional read — what changes is the regime’s answer to the question “under your own calibration, is the trajectory you have observed more or less likely than not?”. Through yesterday the answer was “more likely than not”; from today onward it is “less likely than not.”

What “Asymmetric Surprise Budget” Means

The phrase is structural rather than rhetorical. The post-horizon regime publishes Rule B’s implied per-day P(null) ~0.93 as a calibration baseline, not as a forecast. The baseline is a description of the regime’s own behaviour: given the four-scenario ladder, the four-indicator watch list, the post-eviction natural-experiment rule, and the empirical event distribution since the May 28 horizon, the methodology calibrates against a 93% per-day probability that no watch-list-relevant event fires. The baseline is published so that compound trajectories like the one observed can be scored against the regime’s own calibration without operator discretion at the scoring step.

Through yesterday (June 5, Day 9 post-horizon), the joint probability of the observed all-null trajectory was 0.939 ≈ 52% — modestly more likely than not. Through today (Day 10) it is 0.9310 ≈ 48% — modestly less likely than not. The surprise budget the regime publishes against is structurally asymmetric across this threshold:

  • Before today. Any further null day was, by the regime’s own calibration, the modal outcome — the boring case rather than the surprising one. A watch-list event firing was the surprise; null days were not. The regime’s discipline rule that “extended quiet stretches between methodology-quality transitions are the modal evolution” was a description of the baseline.
  • From today. The marginal cost of each additional null day, in calibration-baseline terms, is non-trivial. The boring case is now (modestly) less likely than not; the surprise case (a watch-list event firing) is now (modestly) more likely than not. The regime’s discipline claim is for the first time under empirical pressure on the calibration side: the architecture continues to publish through, but the rationale page is committing to a sequence of trajectories the regime’s own baseline assigns sub-50% probability to.

The asymmetry is structural rather than directional. The regime does not need to respond to crossing the sub-50% threshold — the calibration baseline is a published series, not a signal the methodology acts on, and the post-horizon discipline rule explicitly disclaims operator discretion at calibration crossings. What it does need to do is continue to publish under the new asymmetric surprise budget, with the dial unchanged, while the calibration baseline continues to compound downward.

Why 0.93 Is Itself a Published Series

The 0.93 per-day P(null) value the regime calibrates against is not an opinion. It is the Rule B implied null-rate computed against the four-indicator post-horizon watch list, the post-eviction empirical event distribution, and the four-scenario architecture inherited from the cliff. The methodology pre-commits to publishing the value rather than carrying it as ex-post commentary because the calibration-quality landmark class is structurally meaningful only if the baseline against which trajectories are scored is observable to the same reader who observes the dial.

Pre-commitment of the calibration baseline produces three structural properties the post-horizon regime depends on. First, the calibration-side surprise budget is a published series that can be falsified: if the regime crosses sub-50% on a trajectory that it had previously characterised as “modal,” the falsifying evidence is a number on the rationale page rather than an operator’s after-the-fact explanation. Second, the asymmetric-surprise threshold is a calendar event, not a judgement: it fires on a published date irrespective of what the dial does, just as the cliff fired on May 20, the revision on May 25, and the horizon on May 28. Third, the regime’s discipline claim that “extended quiet stretches between methodology-quality transitions are the modal evolution” is itself testable against the calibration baseline: a regime that crosses sub-50% on its modal trajectory is one whose discipline claim is, in calibration-baseline terms, holding to within a 0.93n band; a regime that crosses sub-25% on its modal trajectory is one whose discipline claim is starting to fray.

The methodology has nine more days of calibration-baseline runway before the third week closes at 22%. Under the regime’s pre-commitments, the dashboard publishes through all nine without moving the dial, the live ladder, the watch list, or the rolling window. The calibration baseline compounds downward by a published factor of 0.93 per day. The first time the dial moves — if it moves — will be because a watch-list event has fired, not because the calibration baseline has crossed any threshold.

Calibration vs Reference-Frame: The Pair Across Two Days

The pairing of yesterday’s Day 100 war-clock landmark and today’s sub-50% calibration read is structurally informative. Both landmarks are non-procedural — neither flips a refresh procedure, evicts a scenario, or revises a rule. Both are under-the-hood landmarks at the rationale-page level rather than headline-dial-moving events. But they sit on opposite sides of a structural axis the methodology has not previously made explicit.

The Day 100 mark is backward-looking: it counts days against a fixed past event (the Feb 28 war start). The regime publishes through it; the landmark conditions no future architectural behaviour. The 0.9310 sub-50% crossing is forward-looking in a calibration sense: it conditions the regime’s self-reported surprise budget for every subsequent calendar day of the post-horizon trajectory. Yesterday’s landmark was published through with no architectural consequence; today’s landmark is published under, with the calibration baseline now permanently on the asymmetric side of the 50% line until the regime exits the post-horizon configuration.

The two landmarks together demonstrate that the post-horizon regime carries non-procedural landmarks of structurally different temporal orientations. The dual-tracker architecture’s claim that landmarks of different categories have different implications for the published dashboard now extends across both spatial (war-clock vs ceasefire-clock vs anchor-clock) and temporal (backward-looking vs forward-looking) axes. The methodology page’s landmark taxonomy is, as of today, two-dimensional rather than one-dimensional.

What the Sub-50% Crossing Is and Isn’t

The sub-50% crossing is not a falsification event. The regime’s discipline claim that extended quiet stretches are the modal evolution is not falsified by a single sub-50% calibration read — the claim is about behaviour over the full distribution of post-horizon trajectories, not about any particular threshold crossing. A trajectory that produces a 48% joint baseline today is fully consistent with a regime whose modal evolution is extended quiet stretches; the modal stretch is a quiet one, but quiet stretches asymmetrically longer than 0.9310 are not impossible under the baseline — they are, by construction, the upper tail of the regime’s own distribution.

The sub-50% crossing is also not a signal to revise the rule. Rule B remains the single live ladder; the four-scenario architecture remains unchanged; the four-indicator watch list remains unchanged; the 30-day rolling window shifts forward by one day to Jun 6–Jul 6, exactly as it did on each of the prior seventeen post-cliff calendar days. The reversal procedure remains as defined in the single-rule reversal procedure rule: a Rule B-falsifying event (fresh seizure cluster, rupture indicator, or comprehensive deal signal contradicting all-into-stalemate) would require a separately-published methodology-page reversal; in the absence of such an event, the rule stands regardless of where the calibration baseline sits.

What the sub-50% crossing is, structurally, is the first calendar event at which the regime is required to publish a trajectory under an asymmetric surprise budget. The regime continues to publish the dial unchanged at ~6.3%, the ladder unchanged at 70/23/7/0, and the watch list unchanged at four operative indicators. What changes is the implicit cost of each subsequent null day in calibration-baseline terms: the marginal day before today was the modal case; the marginal day from today is the modestly-surprising case. The headline does not move; the rationale page is for the first time committed to a sequence of trajectories the regime’s own baseline assigns sub-50% probability to. The dual-tracker architecture’s sharpest test yet of its own claim that calibration-side movement is observable without dial-side movement begins today.
