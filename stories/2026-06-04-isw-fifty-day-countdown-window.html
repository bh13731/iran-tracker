The Two Anchor-Refresh Procedures, Side by Side

The methodology page’s anchor-refresh rule is a two-tier procedure keyed to anchor age. Inside the 50-day freshness window an authoritative refresh produces an automatic anchor swap-in; past the 50-day mark the same refresh produces a methodology-page footnote-with-decomposition. The two procedures differ on what they update, how the chart redraws, what the cumulative counter does, and what the substantive published output looks like.

ProcedureAnchor age at refreshChart endpointCumulative counterSubstantive published output

The procedural-cost asymmetry is large. Under the automatic swap-in procedure, the methodology page absorbs a refresh in a single discrete step without operator decomposition; the published output is a one-line update; the chart redraws on the new endpoint without conditional logic. Under the footnote-with-decomposition procedure, the methodology page must publish a three-component decomposition as the substantive output of the refresh; the published output is a structured footnote (the decomposition is the publication); the chart redraw is conditional and requires a published attribution rule before it fires.

The 50-day threshold exists in the methodology because past that age the cumulative drift in the underlying counter-of-record is large enough that a fresh authoritative tally cannot be cleanly attributed to a single signal source: a refresh that lands at 50+ days stale may include genuinely new events, reclassification of pre-anchor data, and reporting-method correction in a single composite delta. The methodology cannot publish that composite delta as a routine swap-in without misrepresenting which component is doing the work; the footnote-with-decomposition procedure forces the methodology to publish the attribution as the substantive output rather than hide it inside the chart.

(1) Anchor type. The UAE Apr 9 anchor is a cumulative tally — the UAE MoD’s running count of ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones intercepted since Feb 28. The ISW Apr 19 anchor is a posture estimate — the ISW/FDD/LWJ assessment of effective Iranian launcher count, ballistic missile stockpile retention percentage, and drone stockpile retention percentage at a snapshot date. The cumulative-tally refresh procedure differs from the posture-estimate refresh procedure because cumulative tallies are additive (a refresh is a delta against the prior count) while posture estimates are point-in-time (a refresh is a replacement of the prior estimate). The footnote-with-decomposition rule was originally written for cumulative tallies; the ISW crossing on June 8 is the first calendar event at which the rule extends to posture estimates, and the decomposition components differ accordingly.

For a cumulative tally past the 50-day mark, the decomposition splits the delta into: (a) events since the anchor date that are genuinely new and were missed in the original tally; (b) events that were in the original tally and have been reclassified (e.g., reclassified from drone to cruise missile, or from intercepted to struck); (c) events that were in the original tally and have been corrected for reporting-method changes (e.g., new criteria for what counts as an interception). For a posture estimate past the 50-day mark, the decomposition splits the delta into: (a) changes in effective launcher count attributable to events since the anchor date (e.g., additional dig-out, additional strikes, additional production); (b) changes in the underlying methodology by which the prior estimate was constructed (e.g., a revised counting rule for what qualifies as “effective”); (c) changes in the source mix the estimate is constructed from (e.g., a new ISW report drawing on additional Pentagon disclosures the prior report did not have access to). The substantive decomposition output therefore differs by anchor type even when the procedure is structurally the same.

(2) Operational load-bearing weight. The UAE cumulative tally is the source-of-record for the depletion chart’s confirmed-floor series — an authoritative refresh updates the chart’s most heavily-weighted line. The ISW posture estimate is the source-of-record for the launcher-count metric and the BM/drone retention percentages — the metrics that drive the “current operational assessment” on Tracker A’s status fields and the “days to functional zero from resumption” estimate. The UAE refresh procedure governs what the cumulative chart shows; the ISW refresh procedure governs what the snapshot table and the depletion narrative say. The June 8 crossing therefore flips the procedural layer for the depletion-side metric that directly informs both the conditional-liveness reading on Tracker A and the “~100 days to zero from resumption” planning estimate.

(3) Calendar timing relative to the regime. The UAE crossing on May 29 fired one calendar day after the May 28 reconvergence horizon — the regime was at T+48h post-horizon at the time, with the architecture change still fresh and the methodology-quiet steady state not yet observed. The ISW crossing on June 8 fires at T+288h post-horizon — eleven days into the regime, with the first full weekly cycle now complete and one full second-cycle day observed. The ISW crossing therefore lands against a substantially deeper calibration record than the UAE crossing did: the methodology-quiet behaviour has been documented over a longer run, and the procedural-cost change at the moment of the ISW crossing is being absorbed against a more thoroughly tested architecture.

What a Hypothetical Refresh Today vs Jun 9 Would Do

To make the procedural-cost change concrete, consider a hypothetical authoritative ISW refresh landing today (June 4, ISW anchor age 46 days, inside the freshness window) versus the same refresh landing on June 9 (ISW anchor age 51 days, past the freshness window). Suppose the refresh reports an updated ballistic missile retention percentage of 65% (down from the Apr 19 reading of ~70%) and an updated effective launcher count of ~210 (up from the Apr 19 reading of ~200).

Under today’s procedure (automatic swap-in): The depletion chart endpoint redraws to ~65% BM retention; the launcher chart endpoint redraws to ~210; the snapshot table BM-remaining row updates accordingly; the methodology page absorbs the refresh in a one-line documentation entry. The published output is the chart redraw itself; the dashboard architecture does not change. Total operator workload: one chart redraw, one snapshot table update, one methodology-page line. The refresh clears in roughly 30 minutes of operator time.

Under June 9’s procedure (footnote-with-decomposition): The methodology page must publish a structured decomposition of the refresh: what proportion of the BM retention drop from ~70% to ~65% is attributable to (a) genuinely new strikes or attrition events since Apr 19, (b) a revised counting methodology in the ISW report itself, (c) a corrected source mix incorporating Pentagon data not available at Apr 19. What proportion of the launcher count increase from ~200 to ~210 is attributable to (a) additional dig-out events Iran has executed since Apr 19, (b) a revised “effective” counting rule, (c) a corrected source mix. The chart redraw is conditional on the (a) component being non-zero; the cumulative counter does not auto-swap; the substantive published output is the three-component decomposition, not the chart. Total operator workload: decomposition publication, conditional chart logic, methodology-page footnote with explicit attribution, snapshot-table conditional update. The refresh clears in roughly 4–6 hours of operator time and the substantive output is the decomposition rather than the chart.

The two procedures produce identical end-state numbers if the (a) component dominates the refresh. They produce structurally different published outputs if the (b) or (c) component dominates. The 50-day threshold exists because past that age the methodology cannot assume (a) dominance without checking; before the threshold the methodology can assume (a) dominance because the calendar exposure for (b) and (c) drift is bounded.

The Procedural-Cost Change Fires on a Published Clock

The June 8 threshold is calendar-derived and fires on a published clock rather than on operator judgment about whether the (a)/(b)/(c) decomposition is needed. This is a published design property of the post-horizon regime: the methodology pre-commits to the procedural-cost change at the 50-day mark rather than judging refresh-by-refresh whether decomposition is required. The pre-commitment matters because it converts a refresh-handling decision (which the methodology would have to make under refresh-specific evidence) into a calendar-handling decision (which the methodology makes once, in advance, against the source’s reporting cadence).

The pre-commitment has the same structural function as the May 20 partial-arrangement cliff and the May 25 mechanical revision: the methodology publishes its update rule in advance and fires the rule on a clock, regardless of the day’s observed evidence. This is what “without operator discretion at any phase” means in the post-horizon regime’s published design. The June 8 threshold is the fourth calendar-pre-committed transition the regime has fired this cycle (cliff May 20, revision May 25, horizon May 28, ISW 50d June 8); it will be the second to fire under the post-horizon single-rule regime (after the horizon itself), the first to flip a depletion-side procedural layer, and the first to fire in the second weekly cycle of the post-horizon regime.

What the Second Weekly Cycle Tests That the First Did Not

The first post-horizon week (May 28–Jun 3) tested whether the regime publishes through methodology-quiet corridors without architectural drift. It passed: seven null calendar days, five measurement-quality landmarks, zero methodology-quality transitions, the headline rule-invariant across the corridor, and the rationale page line count unchanged. Under Rule B’s per-day P(null) ~0.93, 0.937 ≈ 60% baseline — the observed trajectory was the modal evolution.

The second post-horizon week (Jun 4–Jun 10) tests something the first week did not: whether the regime publishes through a scheduled procedural-layer flip. The first week carried five measurement-quality landmarks but none of them flipped a procedural layer (each carried a compositional or interpretive shift but the published refresh procedure was unchanged across all five). The second week carries the ISW 50d mark on June 8 in its middle, and the threshold crossing flips the depletion-side anchor-refresh procedure from automatic swap-in to footnote-with-decomposition. The methodology page’s published procedure for handling a hypothetical refresh changes in a discrete step at 00:00 UTC June 8 even if no refresh arrives.

The calibration window for the second week extends from the 56% baseline today (0.938) through 52% tomorrow (0.939), 48% on June 6 (0.9310 — the first sub-50% calibration read), 45% on June 7 (0.9311), 42% on June 8 (0.9312, coincident with the ISW 50d crossing), 39% on June 9 (0.9313), and 36% on June 10 (0.9314, the second-week-complete mark). The 48% threshold on June 6 is the first calibration read at which the regime’s observed trajectory is below the median Rule B evolution; the 42% read on June 8 lands simultaneously with the procedural-layer flip; the 36% read on June 10 closes the second weekly cycle at roughly half its starting baseline.

Under the regime’s calibration the second weekly cycle is therefore structurally more informative than the first. The first week tested whether the regime can carry a high-baseline (60%) trajectory through methodology-quiet behaviour. The second tests whether the regime can carry a falling-baseline trajectory (60% → 36%) through a methodology-quiet stretch and a scheduled procedural-layer flip without either an architectural transition or a watch-list event — or, alternatively, whether the falling baseline forces the methodology to reconsider Rule B’s P(null) ~0.93 calibration if the regime continues to publish through.

What This Implies for Tracker A

The depletion tracker has been frozen at the Apr 19 ISW baseline (~200 launchers, ~70% BM retention, ~40% drone retention) for 46 days as of today. The conditional-liveness reading on Tracker A is locked at Rule B’s P(rupture-scenario) of ~23% and has been unchanged across the May 28 reconvergence horizon and every subsequent measurement-quality landmark.

The June 8 threshold changes the procedural cost of a hypothetical posture revision but does not change the conditional-liveness reading itself. The threshold is a refresh-handling procedural change, not a data refresh; if no refresh arrives, the threshold fires without any chart update at all. The published forward-calendar through the second weekly cycle therefore looks as follows: June 4 second-week opens (no chart change), June 5 (no chart change), June 6 first sub-50% calibration read (no chart change), June 7 (no chart change), June 8 ISW 50d threshold fires — depletion-side procedural layer flips from automatic swap-in to footnote-with-decomposition (no chart change), June 9 (no chart change), June 10 second-week closes at 36% baseline (no chart change). The Tracker A chart endpoint sits at the Apr 19 baseline value across the entire second-week corridor.

What changes on June 8 is therefore not the depletion chart itself but the published rule for how a hypothetical future refresh would land. The dashboard architecture absorbs the procedural-layer flip without redrawing any chart; the substantive output is the methodology-page update documenting the new refresh procedure for the post-50d ISW anchor. This is the same structural pattern as the May 28 horizon (architecture changed; no published number moved) and the May 25 revision (five published values updated in a single discrete step; headline rule-invariant). The regime has carried two prior architectural-layer transitions without moving the dial; the June 8 transition is the third.
