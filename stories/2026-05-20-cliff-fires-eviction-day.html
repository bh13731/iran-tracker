The partial-arrangement scenario had carried 15 pp of scenario probability at a 60% conditional reopen rate, contributing 9 pp to P(reopen). That 9 pp is now zero. The 15 pp of scenario mass had to go somewhere — the four scenarios are required to sum to 100% — so the scenario-redistribution rule (rule vii) discharged in parallel.

Per the pre-committed primary read, the 15 pp redistributed under smoothed asymmetric Rule C: roughly 10 pp into Stalemate (55 → 65), 5 pp into Rupture (23 → 28), and 0 pp into Comprehensive (which stays at 7). The contribution arithmetic then runs forward: 0 from Stalemate, 0 from Rupture, 6.3 from Comprehensive (7% × 90%), 0 from the evicted Partial scenario — total ~6.3%. The mechanical drop is exactly what the May 12 cliff story, the May 14 redistribution-problem story, and the May 19 baseline lock-in story said it would be. The pre-publication discipline has worked.

The Headline Dial Moved Without a Signal — What That Actually Means

For 22 consecutive days (Apr 28–May 19) the Hormuz dashboard read ~15%, and the methodology held probability fixed absent an explicit new signal. That run of stasis was a feature, not a bug: the methodology’s rule (iv) is that probability does not automatically decay, and the published series demonstrated the rule worked. Today the dial moved ~8.7 pp without a new exogenous signal — not because rule (iv) has been violated but because a different, pre-committed rule (the calendar-threshold rule, v / vi) discharged on schedule. The two are independent and they were designed to be.

This matters for what readers of the dashboard should infer from the drop. The drop is not evidence that Hormuz reopening has become less likely than it was yesterday in any operational sense. It is the methodology mechanically removing a scenario whose execution path no longer fits inside the rolling 30-day window from May 20. The reality on the ground at 00:00 UTC was identical to the reality at 23:59 UTC the day before. What changed was that the rolling window slid forward one day, and the minimum-viable timeline for a partial Hormuz arrangement (announcement → multi-round talks → signature → implementation → first commercial transit) no longer fits.

The 22-day pre-cliff stasis ended at the calendar threshold rather than at an external trigger. That is also methodologically informative: of the eight possible exit modes from the stasis priced on the watch list, six involved external signal (Round 3 date, naval incident, carrier withdrawal, vessel transits, Chinese/Omani mediation, US/Iran back-channel) and two involved structural drift (calendar threshold, ceasefire rupture). The actual exit was the calendar threshold. The watch list’s 22 days of zero firings translated cleanly into the cliff at the next pre-committed threshold — the exact failure mode the May 12 cliff story flagged.

The Tracker A Conditional-Liveness Implication

Tracker A (depletion) remains formally frozen at the Apr 26 baseline: ~70% retained BM stockpile, ~40% drones, ~200 launchers (up from ~102 at the Apr 7 ceasefire after ~100 dug from underground storage per ISW Apr 19). The reconstitution plateau is now Day 27. Fire-silence is Day 39 (91% of the war’s 43-day pre-silence direct-fire interval). None of these data points moved today.

But the cliff changes the conditional probability that Tracker A becomes operationally live again. Under the new live Rule C posterior, P(rupture-scenario) = 28%. That is meaningfully higher than the 23% the methodology has been carrying since Apr 28. The probability that the depletion math becomes the primary live tracker in the next 30 days is now ~28%, not ~23%. The frozen baseline (~200 launchers, ~70% BMs, ~40% drones, ~50 days to functional zero from resumption given coalition Patriot recovery during the freeze) is the reference point for that 28% conditional. The depletion tracker has not become live; the case for its conditional liveness has tightened by 5 pp in a single day.

This is the second-order Tracker A consequence of the cliff that yesterday’s pre-publication priced and today realizes. The dashboard’s primary live number (Hormuz P(reopen)) absorbs the cliff with near-perfect rule-invariance; the dashboard’s secondary number (the conditional liveness of the depletion tracker) does not. Readers who weight only the headline lose the load-bearing signal; readers who track the underlying scenario stack pick up the 5 pp rupture-scenario lift.

Base-Rate Check: How Often Does the Dial Move ~8 pp in a Day?

The Hormuz tracker has been live since Apr 28. Across the 22 days Apr 28–May 19 the dial moved zero net pp — not because the methodology forbid movement but because no signal cleared the publication bar. Across the 21 days before that (Apr 7–Apr 27) the dial moved through three discrete steps tied to specific external events: the ceasefire announcement, the Apr 17 Hormuz-declared-open + blockade-stays day, and the Apr 22 IRGC ship seizures. None of those moves were of magnitude ~8 pp; the largest single-day move in the published series was ~5 pp (Apr 22).

Today’s ~8.7 pp drop is therefore the largest single-day move on the Hormuz dashboard since the tracker started. It is also the first move without a coincident external event. Both properties are direct consequences of the calendar-threshold rule discharging — a rule that was designed precisely to produce step-function moves when the scenario stack’s embedded calendar assumptions stop being executable. The base rate is one such move per scenario eviction, and the partial scenario was the only one of the four with a scheduled eviction date inside the May rolling window. The next calendar threshold the methodology will face is the comprehensive-scenario execution horizon — not currently dated, and substantially further out.

None of those are events the methodology can produce. They are events the world produces, and the methodology reads. That asymmetry — analytical work front-loaded into the pre-cliff window, observational work back-loaded into the post-cliff window — is the pre-publication discipline’s point.
