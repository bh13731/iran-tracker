The Surprise Budget Crosses Sub-30%: When the Calibration Starts to Question Its Own Event Rate

June 13, 2026 — Day 67 Analysis Cease-fire Calibration quality Sub-30% surprise budget Bayesian self-test
At 00:00 UTC June 13 the post-horizon regime crosses a round number that lives entirely on the rationale page. Under Rule B’s implied per-day P(null) ≈ 0.93, the joint probability of seventeen consecutive null post-horizon days is 0.9317 ≈ 29% — the first sub-30% calibration read of the regime, and the eighth consecutive day under what the dashboard calls its asymmetric surprise budget. P(Hormuz reopen 30d) = ~6.3% (Rule B, single live ladder), unchanged for the twenty-fifth consecutive day; the dial does not move. But for the first time the regime’s own calibration is saying something sharp enough to be worth saying plainly: the observed quiet is now, by the methodology’s own number, less than one-in-three likely — and the right response is not to brace for an event but to ask whether the model’s event rate was ever right. What the 0.93n Series Actually Is

The calibration baseline is not a forecast of P(reopen). It is a published bookkeeping series with one job: to state, in advance, how surprised the methodology should be by its own silence. Rule B’s posture implies that on any given post-horizon day the modal outcome is a null — no fresh seizure, no release, no commercial-transit cluster, no anchor revision, no rupture indicator, no Round 3 announcement, no proxy-escalation cluster, no comprehensive-deal signal — with an implied per-day probability of roughly 0.93. Compounding that across n consecutive null days gives 0.93n: the prior probability, set on May 28, of seeing an unbroken quiet stretch this long.

The threshold ladder. The series has now crossed three round numbers in eight days. 0.939 ≈ 52% (Jun 5, first sub-55%) → 0.9310 ≈ 48% (Jun 6, first sub-50% — the surprise budget opens) → 0.9314 ≈ 37% (Jun 10, first sub-40%) → 0.9317 ≈ 29% (Jun 13, first sub-30%). Each crossing is a calibration-quality landmark: it does not move the dial, the four-scenario ladder, the four-indicator watch list, or the rolling window. What it moves is the methodology’s self-reported surprise level — and at sub-30% that level has crossed from “modal” through “unremarkable” into “mildly improbable.”

Six weeks ago the all-null trajectory was the boring case — the thing the regime expected to publish. The 0.93n series is the instrument that lets you watch the boring case stop being boring without pretending the dial changed. As of today the boring case has a 29% prior. That is the entire content of the landmark, and it is worth being careful about what follows from it — because there are two tempting misreadings and only one correct one.

The likelihood ratio is the whole story: seventeen nulls are about twice as likely under a 0.97 daily null rate as under 0.93, and roughly three times as likely under 0.99. A flat prior over p would, after seventeen nulls, put its posterior mass distinctly above 0.93. In plain terms: the regime’s own silence is mounting evidence that the methodology slightly over-estimated how often watch-list events fire under this ceasefire architecture — that the post-horizon stalemate is stickier than the May-28 calibration assumed. The surprise budget is not measuring rising tension. It is measuring its own miscalibration, in the only direction the data can push it: toward quieter.

Why a Stickier Stalemate Still Doesn’t Move the Dial

If the honest inference is “the true null rate is higher than 0.93,” the natural next question is whether that should change P(reopen). It should — by a fraction of a point, in the bearish direction, well inside the ±8 pp band the methodology already publishes around the estimate. Here is the mechanism, and why it nets to almost nothing.

A higher per-day null rate is, almost definitionally, evidence for the Stalemate scenario — the world in which the blockade holds and nothing resolves. Under Rule B that scenario already carries 70% and contributes 0 pp to P(reopen). Marginal weight flowing into Stalemate has to come out of the other three scenarios — and the only one of those that contributes anything to the dial is the 7% Comprehensive deal (the lone positive-side path, at P(open | comprehensive) = 90%). So a stickier-stalemate update nudges a sliver of probability away from the one scenario that feeds P(reopen):

That is the architecture working exactly as intended. The same property that makes the dial insulated from rupture risk — that the two largest scenarios both contribute 0 pp — also makes it insulated from calibration drift. A stickier stalemate is bearish for reopening only through the thin Comprehensive channel, and the effect is far too small to round the headline off ~6.3%. The calibration baseline can fall to single digits without the dial owing it a move; the only thing that would genuinely re-rate P(reopen) is an event on the watch list, and the surprise budget is precisely the published record that no such event has come.

The clean statement. Sub-30% is the first calibration read at which the regime’s silence is mildly improbable under its own event-rate model. The correct inference is not “an event is overdue” (the days are independent) and not “the dashboard is broken” (the dial is insulated by design), but “the 0.93 per-day null rate was, if anything, set too high a fire rate.” That re-estimate is weakly bearish for reopening through the single Comprehensive channel and quantitatively negligible against the ±8 pp band — so the dial holds at ~6.3% for the twenty-fifth straight day while the rationale page records the model, not the world, being put under test.

What moved today is the regime’s confidence that its own clock was ticking at the right speed — and the direction of the doubt is toward a stalemate even stickier than the dashboard already prices.
