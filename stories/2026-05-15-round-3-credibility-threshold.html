The Round 3 Credibility Threshold: What an Announcement Would Need to Look Like to Avert the May 20 Cliff

May 15, 2026 — Day 38 Analysis Cease-fire Hormuz
Day 38 of cease-fire. P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, static for 17 consecutive days. Five days remain to the May 20 partial-arrangement cliff. The two prior stories priced what happens when the cliff fires — the redistribution rule that determines the post-eviction scenario ladder, and the joint probability that any single watch-list event averts it. Today prices the positive-side escape valve at its source: what would a Round 3 announcement actually have to contain to count as the cliff-averting signal under the methodology? Unpacking the headline phrase into five concrete credibility criteria reveals that the unconditional ~5% joint 5-day probability of a “Round 3 mentioned” headline collapses to ~2–3% conditional on the credibility test passing. The cliff is near-certain to fire on schedule, and the headline-quality bar required to forestall it is materially higher than the watch-list’s nominal +5–10 pp trigger value implies.

The Asymmetry the Watch List Hides

The watch list assigns the “Islamabad Round 3 date announced (credible)” indicator a +5–10 pp expected impact on P(reopen). The parenthetical is doing nearly all the analytical work in that line. A non-credible Round 3 announcement — a Trump press scrum mentioning “talks soon,” a Pakistan Foreign Ministry statement that “Round 3 is being scheduled,” an Iranian state-media claim of US backchannel openness — would generate the headline but fail the methodology’s avert condition. The cliff would still fire on schedule, because the partial-arrangement scenario’s P(open | scenario) is set by whether a viable execution path exists inside the rolling 30-day window, not by whether a headline exists. Today’s analytical problem is to specify what makes a Round 3 announcement credible in the methodology’s operational sense, and then to count how likely each credibility criterion is to fire in the remaining five days given 17 days of watch-list silence.

The Five Credibility Criteria

A Round 3 announcement averts the May 20 cliff only if it meets all five of the following criteria. Failing any single one converts the announcement from cliff-averting into watch-list noise that may shift P(scenario) by 1–3 pp but does not change the P(open | scenario) floor.

# Criterion What “passing” means Base rate in 5 days (est)
i Named date A specific calendar date or an unambiguous window (e.g., “May 28”, “the week of May 26”) — not “soon,” “coming weeks,” or “in due course” ~10–15%
ii Named host / format A specific host city (Islamabad, Muscat, Doha) and a confirmed bilateral or multilateral format — not “diplomatic channels” or unspecified “mediated talks” ~50–70%
iii Named Iranian delegation lead An identified senior official with the standing to negotiate (Araghchi, Ghalibaf, Mohammadi at minister-level) — not “an Iranian delegation” or working-level staff ~30–40%
iv Named US-side participation An identified US official at minimum deputy-secretary level or designated envoy — not “representatives” or undefined State Department staff ~25–35%
v Executable inside ~22-day stack The announced date plus the empirical execution timeline (Round 1 took ~14 days announcement-to-meeting; Round 2 was on a ~22-day stack) leaves a remaining window before Jun 14 wide enough for the partial-arrangement scenario to operationally close ~50–70%

The criteria are partially correlated — a credible announcement with named delegates is more likely also to carry a named date — but they are not perfectly correlated. A US presidential press remark could clear (iv) but fail (i)–(iii). An Iranian Foreign Ministry statement could clear (i)–(iii) but fail (iv). The joint base rate for all five clearing simultaneously in the next 5 days, treating the criteria as moderately correlated (correlation coefficient roughly 0.4 across pairs), lands at ~2–3%. Treating them as fully independent would collapse the joint probability to well below 1%; treating them as perfectly correlated would lift it toward the ~10–15% range of the weakest single criterion. The 2–3% figure is the moderate-correlation central case.

The diagnostic value of this decomposition is the gap between the unconditional and conditional figures. A media-monitoring approach that treats “any Round 3 mention in the next 5 days” as the watch-list trigger would overstate the cliff-aversion probability by a factor of 2–2.5x. The methodology’s credibility filter is what produces the lower conditional figure, and the lower figure is the right one to use for forecasting whether P(reopen) on May 21 will be ~15% (cliff averted) or ~6–8% (cliff fired).

The Historical Calibration: What Rounds 1 and 2 Looked Like

The five criteria are not abstract. Both Round 1 (early April) and Round 2 (mid-April, postponed) generated announcements that can be back-tested against the credibility filter:

(iv) Named US-side participationYes — named envoy / Vance attachedYes — VP Vance named

Both prior rounds met all five criteria at the announcement stage. Round 2 nevertheless failed at execution — Iran did not show, the 10-point frame collapsed, and the round was postponed indefinitely. The empirical lesson: even passing the credibility test is necessary but not sufficient for the partial-arrangement scenario to actually execute. The methodology’s P(open | scenario) of 60% for the partial scenario already prices that the announcement is only the first hurdle. The cliff-aversion question is the narrower one: does a credible announcement exist before May 20? Round 1 and Round 2 each cleared that bar in their respective announcement windows. The current 17-day silence and the operational deadlock around Hormuz make a comparable announcement in the next 5 days a low-base-rate event.

What this means for the watch list’s +5–10 pp trigger value: The watch-list line item “Islamabad Round 3 date announced (credible) → +5–10 pp” should be read as conditional on the credibility filter, not unconditional on a headline. A non-credible announcement is a 1–3 pp event, not a 5–10 pp event. The watch list’s nominal +5–10 pp value is correct, but the base rate that produces a +5–10 pp outcome is the conditional ~2–3% figure, not the unconditional ~5% figure.

What a Round 3 Announcement in the Next 5 Days Probably Looks Like If It Happens

If a Round 3 announcement does emerge between May 15 and May 20, the most likely modal shape — conditional on its occurring at all — is a Pakistan Foreign Office statement naming a date in late May or early June, naming Islamabad as host, naming Ghalibaf or Araghchi on the Iranian side, but leaving the US-side participation deliberately vague (“US representatives” or “US officials,” without naming a level). That announcement would clear (i), (ii), (iii), and (v), but fail (iv). Under the methodology’s strict credibility test, the cliff still fires.

This shape is what Pakistani mediators have produced in both prior rounds: a Pakistan-led announcement that names everything except the US-side commitment, with the US-side commitment delivered by Washington in a subsequent statement (or, in Round 2’s case, withdrawn before execution). The 22-day execution stack and the empirical base rate of US-side participation announcements lagging Iranian-side announcements by 24–72 hours mean that the canonical sequence is: Pakistan-led headline + Iranian-side confirmation, then a 1–3 day gap, then US-side participation either confirmed or quietly demurred. In the 5-day window, that sequence runs to the wire.

The structural conclusion: even in the modal positive-path world — one where a Round 3 announcement does materialize — the most likely sequence has the credible US-side confirmation arriving on May 19 or May 20, with non-trivial probability of arriving on May 21 or later. The cliff fires anyway in the “US-side confirmation lands a day late” branch.

The Partial-Credibility Path: What If Some Criteria Clear But Not All

The cleaner case is binary: either all five criteria clear and the cliff is averted, or one or more fail and the cliff fires. But the methodology operates with continuous P(scenario) values, so a partial-credibility outcome has a calibrated effect. The mapping the tracker uses for partial-credibility events:

Outcome Effect on P(partial scenario) Effect on P(open | partial) Headline P(reopen) shift
All 5 criteria clear (credible announcement) +5 pp (15→20%) Held at 60% +3 pp (15→18%); cliff averted
4 of 5 clear (modal positive case) +2–3 pp (15→17–18%) Mechanical floor still applies on May 21 +1–2 pp pre-cliff; cliff still fires; post-cliff base ~7–8%
3 of 5 clear (vague Round 3 mention) +1 pp (15→16%) Mechanical floor applies ~0–1 pp pre-cliff; cliff fires; post-cliff base ~6.5%
0–2 of 5 clear (base case) No change Mechanical floor applies 0 pp; cliff fires; post-cliff base ~6.3–7.4%

The middle two rows of this table are operationally the most important. They describe what happens when a Round 3 announcement materializes but does not quite clear the credibility bar — the most likely positive-side outcome conditional on anything happening at all. In those cases the headline P(reopen) bumps by 1–2 pp in the days before the cliff, and then the cliff fires anyway on May 20, with the partial scenario’s mass redistributing per the published smoothed Rule C. The net headline trajectory looks like “15% → 17% → ~8%” over a 5–6 day window, rather than “15% → ~7%” under the base case.

What This Means for May 16–May 20

Three operational consequences for how the next five days should be read:

  • Discount Round 3 headlines that do not name a US-side participant. The default shape of a positive-side surprise in the next 5 days is a Pakistan-led announcement with vague US-side language. Treat such a headline as a +1–2 pp event, not a +5–10 pp event. The expected market reaction (oil prices, shipping insurance) may overshoot, providing a brief negative-EV window for the headline P(reopen) before the methodology re-anchors.
  • Watch for the US-side confirmation cycle, not the Iran-side announcement cycle. The credibility-determining variable is (iv), and (iv) is the criterion most likely to arrive late or vague. Track the cadence of White House / State Department statements about Iran from May 16 onward; if (iv) does not clear by May 19, the cliff fires regardless of what Iran or Pakistan have said.
  • The negative-side asymmetry is large. A naval-incident or proxy-attack event in the same 5-day window carries a −5 to −8 pp watch-list value at full credibility — and there is no comparable five-criterion credibility filter on rupture-side events. A second IRGC seizure is unambiguously a seizure; the credibility filter on negative events is essentially binary (the event happened or it didn’t). The asymmetry of the credibility test between positive and negative watch-list triggers is large, and it skews the expected-value 5-day path further downward than the headline trigger values alone suggest.

The Frozen Tracker’s Equivalent Question

The depletion tracker (Tracker A) does not have a credibility filter problem, because it does not run on probabilities. Its credibility filter is the source-tier hierarchy: a Reuters story is Tier 3 and does not move the floor; a UAE MoD cumulative is Tier 1 and moves the floor immediately. The Hormuz tracker (Tracker B) is structurally noisier because it operates over forward-looking diplomatic claims rather than backward-looking intercept counts. The Round 3 credibility test is the Hormuz tracker’s analog of the source-tier hierarchy — a way to convert a category-uncertain claim (“Round 3 announced”) into a calibrated effect on P(scenario). It is doing the same epistemic work the source-tier hierarchy does on Tracker A, applied to a class of evidence (diplomatic announcements) that is intrinsically harder to tier.

The 26-day anchor-data lag on Tracker A (ISW Apr 19) and the 36-day lag on the last Gulf cumulative (UAE Apr 9) are the depletion tracker’s equivalent of the Hormuz tracker’s watch-list silence. Both lags are evidence of a stable equilibrium — if either tracker had moving variables, the data would be fresher. The structural symmetry is that both trackers are reporting from the same underlying state: Day 38 of a holding ceasefire with no active diplomatic or operational track. The credibility filter is what lets the Hormuz tracker hold its probabilities constant in the absence of credible signal, rather than in the absence of any signal.

Bottom Line

P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, unchanged for 17 consecutive days. Five days remain to the methodology-defined May 20 partial-arrangement cliff. A credible Round 3 announcement would forestall the cliff; the five credibility criteria — named date, named host, named Iranian lead, named US-side participation, executable inside the 22-day stack — collectively clear with a 5-day joint probability of ~2–3%, materially below the ~5% unconditional probability of any Round 3 headline.

The modal positive-path outcome, conditional on anything happening in the next 5 days, is a Pakistan-led announcement that names every party except the US side — clearing four criteria and failing the fifth. That outcome bumps the partial-arrangement scenario probability by 2–3 pp pre-cliff and adds 1–2 pp to the headline P(reopen), but does not avert the cliff. The cliff fires anyway on May 20 and the redistribution proceeds.

The negative-side watch list does not run through a comparable credibility filter, and the asymmetry between positive-side and negative-side triggers in the next 5 days skews the expected-value path for P(reopen) further downward than the watch list’s symmetric trigger values imply. The methodology’s precision lies in the credibility filter, not in the trigger values themselves — and the credibility filter is asymmetric by construction, because positive events require coordinated multi-party signal while negative events require only the unilateral action of a single party with a loaded instrument.
