The Blockade Architecture: Why Hormuz Stays Closed Even When Missiles Stop Flying

April 28, 2026 — Day 62 Analysis Cease-fire Hormuz
Twenty-one days of fire-silence and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed to normal commercial traffic. The reason is structural: the US blockade is explicitly tied to a comprehensive peace deal, not to the ceasefire. Iran's full compliance with the truce cannot unlock Hormuz. The IRGC's April 22 ship seizures are Tehran's answer to this decoupling. A data-driven breakdown of the 15% reopening probability and what would actually move it.

The Decoupling

On April 17 — Day 10 of the ceasefire — Iran's Foreign Minister Araghchi announced that the Strait of Hormuz was “completely open” for commercial vessels during the truce. Trump responded the same day: the US naval blockade “will remain in full force until a peace deal is reached.”

That exchange defined the structural problem. The ceasefire governs Iran's direct fire. The blockade governs Hormuz access. They are different agreements, controlled by different parties, conditioned on different things. The ceasefire requires Iran to stop shooting. The blockade requires a comprehensive peace. Those are not the same condition, and no amount of fire-silence closes the gap.

The practical result, confirmed by BBC vessel-tracking data: few commercial ships are moving through the Strait. The blockade is real, not rhetorical. Iran has said the words; the US has not moved a ship.

Iran's Answer: The Naval Arm

On April 22, the same day Islamabad Round 2 was postponed indefinitely, the IRGC Navy seized two commercial vessels near the Strait — the first naval provocation since the truce began. The timing was not coincidence.

Iran's Aerospace Force — the missile and drone arm — is grounded. It has not fired directly since April 11. Its launchers are regenerating (~200 estimated operational now, from ~102 at ceasefire), but its political authorization to fire is suspended. The IRGC Navy is not grounded. It can seize ships, harass traffic, and create incidents below the missile threshold without formally breaching the ceasefire.

This is Iran's split-signal architecture in its naval form: the Aerospace Force observes the truce, the IRGC Navy signals coercive intent, the diplomatic corps holds talks (or doesn't). All three tracks run simultaneously, each with deniability or political legitimacy. The ship seizures signal something specific: Iran is not going to allow the US to pocket the fire-silence while keeping the blockade in place.

The stalemate scenario at 55% is the base case. It requires no positive action by either side — just continuation of the current impasse. The ceasefire continues, the blockade holds, and both parties manage the friction at the margins (proxy attacks, ship seizures, carrier deployments). Nothing moves on Hormuz.

The rupture scenario at 23% reflects the elevated fragility of the ceasefire. Proxy attacks on Kuwait (Apr 24), IRGC ship seizures, and zero diplomatic progress all keep this risk elevated. A rupture reduces P(reopen) to near zero in the near term as the Aerospace Force restarts and the US tightens the blockade.

The 15% partial arrangement probability is doing most of the reopening probability's work (9 of the 15 pp). This scenario requires the US to agree to some form of commercial traffic arrangement without a comprehensive deal — perhaps a flagged-vessel arrangement, a temporary suspension of blockade enforcement, or a back-channel via Oman or the UAE. There is no current evidence such a negotiation is underway, but it is structurally possible without requiring full Iran-US peace. It is the path of least political resistance for both sides.

What Would Actually Move the Needle

The probability is not equally sensitive to all possible developments. High-impact watch items, ranked by expected probability shift:

  • Islamabad Round 3 date announced (credible): +5–10 pp. The absence of a Round 3 date is the single largest structural block. A credible date signals both parties see value in continuing — and creates a forcing function for the partial-arrangement path.
  • Second IRGC ship seizure: −5–8 pp per incident. Each seizure raises the rupture probability, signals Iran is not interested in informal accommodation, and makes it harder for the US to offer a partial arrangement without appearing to reward coercion.
  • China or Oman direct mediation on Hormuz specifically: +4–7 pp. The Hormuz question may be more susceptible to third-party arrangements than the comprehensive deal question. Oman has historically served as the Iran-US back channel; China has economic incentives to see oil flow.
  • US carrier withdrawal or visible force drawdown: +3–5 pp. Would signal willingness to ease pressure without requiring a comprehensive deal first. Currently going the opposite direction (3rd carrier ordered).
  • Ceasefire rupture: −13–15 pp immediately. The Aerospace Force restarts; Hormuz threat rises; US tightens blockade. P(reopen 30d) approaches zero.

The Asymmetry of Waiting

The stalemate is not neutral for both sides. Iran's oil exports are heavily constrained under the blockade; revenues are depressed. Its BM production remains halted (assessed since Mar 29). The economic cost compounds daily. The US, by contrast, has limited direct costs from the blockade in energy terms (US is a net oil exporter), and benefits from Iran's accelerating economic pressure.

The coalition also benefits on interceptors: JINSA estimated Gulf Patriot stocks at ~25% on Day 40. Twenty-one days of fire-silence later, those stocks are recovering. Iran's BM inventory cannot grow, but its launchers are recovering (~200 now vs ~102 at ceasefire). The two sides are recovering on different axes — Iran on delivery systems, the coalition on interceptors — with Hormuz providing the economic squeeze on Iran's side.

This asymmetry is why the US has little incentive to ease the blockade without a deal. Time and economic pressure work for the coalition in a stalemate. Iran must either absorb the cost indefinitely or find a diplomatic path that looks like something other than capitulation. That path does not currently exist.

Bottom Line

P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%. The blockade is structurally decoupled from the ceasefire and explicitly tied to a comprehensive deal with no visible path forward. The IRGC's ship seizures signal that Iran's naval coercion will continue below the missile threshold. The most probable outcome through late May is continued stalemate: ceasefire holds, blockade holds, proxy friction continues, both sides absorb the asymmetric cost of waiting.

The probability shifts most dramatically on two events: a credible Islamabad Round 3 date (strong upward pressure) or a second IRGC ship seizure (strong downward pressure). Both are possible within the next week. Watch those two indicators.
