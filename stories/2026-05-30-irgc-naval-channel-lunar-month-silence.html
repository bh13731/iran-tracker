What a Falsification Indicator Does When It Stays Silent

The IRGC Naval channel’s read changed at the May 25 methodology revision. Under Rule C (smoothed asymmetric, primary May 20–24), a fresh IRGC seizure cluster would have corroborated the rule’s rupture-weighted redistribution. Under Rule B (all-into-stalemate, primary since 00:00 UTC May 25 and single live ladder since 00:00 UTC May 28), the same event would falsify the rule and force a separately-published methodology-page reversal re-introducing Rule C at the post-event posterior weights. The calibrated probability impact is preserved across the revision (−3 to −5 pp per IRGC cluster); the epistemic interpretation is inverted.

A corroboration indicator’s silence and a falsification indicator’s silence carry different informational weights. A corroboration indicator that stays silent is weak evidence against the rule it would corroborate — the rule predicts the indicator should fire, and its non-firing accumulates as small adverse Bayesian updates. A falsification indicator that stays silent is weak evidence for the rule it would falsify — the rule predicts the indicator should not fire, and its non-firing each day compounds as small confirming updates against the alternatives. Day 38 of silence under Rule B is therefore an extended sequence of small confirmations, not the kind of waiting-for-a-fire-that-never-comes situation that characterized the May 21–24 natural-experiment window under Rule C.

The day-by-day mathematics is unspectacular: Rule B’s implied per-day P(null) on the IRGC Naval channel is ~0.97 (one event in ~33 days is the empirical 2026-conflict prior, tightened to the Apr 22 single-event prior post-cliff). A 38-day silence under Rule B has joint probability ~0.30 — well inside the modal evolution. A 38-day silence under a counterfactual rule that put per-day P(fresh cluster) at ~0.05 (the Rule C reading at the cliff) has joint probability ~0.14 — possible but in the lower tail. The compounded evidence ratio shifts the rule-posterior toward Rule B as the days accumulate; that shift has already been published into the headline through the May 25 revision and locked in at the May 28 horizon. What today adds is not a fresh rule update — the comparator triplet is retired — but a continued compounding of the structural prior that the Apr 22 event was a single-event cluster rather than the opening of a pattern.

Single-Event Leverage Trap vs Escalation-Pattern Opening Salvo

The Apr 22 seizure cluster, taken on its own day, was ambiguous between two structurally different interpretations. The escalation-pattern opening salvo reading: IRGC took the seizures as a calibrated probe to test US response under the new ceasefire architecture; the response (no kinetic retaliation, no diplomatic concession) revealed acceptable escalation room; IRGC would follow with further seizures or related naval incidents over the following weeks. The single-event leverage trap reading: IRGC took the seizures as a one-time leverage instrument to hold against the comprehensive-deal track; the instrument is reversible (vessels can be released) and lockable (no automatic escalation pressure); IRGC would hold the leverage indefinitely without deploying or escalating, accepting that the instrument loses informational value over time.

At Day 38 the two readings are no longer informationally symmetric. The escalation-pattern reading required at least one of: a fresh seizure cluster within the first ~21 days (gone — the median inter-seizure interval in escalation patterns is sharply tighter than this); a related naval incident in the same theater (gone — the IRGC Navy’s Day 37 silent record has held across the cliff, the revision, the horizon, and the UAE 50-day mark); an articulated demand attached to the held vessels (gone — no diplomatic demand has been issued, beyond the Mar 22 initial statement). The single-event leverage-trap reading is consistent with everything observed. The structural Bayesian update at Day 38 is therefore that the prior on “Apr 22 was a one-off” has compounded above the prior on “Apr 22 was the opening salvo of a pattern.”

This is not a published dial movement — the four-scenario architecture does not break out leverage-trap vs escalation-pattern interpretations of the same channel. But it is a load-bearing shift in the composition of why Rule B’s 70% Stalemate scenario sits where it does. The Stalemate scenario’s probability mass has historically been carried in part by “rupture has not happened yet”, in part by “Iran has revealed a strategic preference for the ceasefire architecture”, and in part by “Iran’s known leverage instruments (Apr 22 vessels, proxy escalation, fire-resumption optionality) remain unused.” The third component is the one Day 38 specifically reweights: an unused leverage instrument at Day 38 is structurally less likely to be deployed than at Day 7 or Day 14, because the cost-of-deployment has grown (informational depreciation, increased risk that the deployment reads as desperation rather than calibrated signal).

What the Channel Would Cost the Methodology Now

Under the post-horizon single-rule regime, a Rule B-falsifying event on the IRGC Naval channel does not produce an automatic in-rule posterior update. The procedural cost is a separately-published methodology-page reversal re-introducing Rule C as primary at the post-event posterior weights (or, in extremis, the introduction of a new rule). The change of update protocol was the substantive cost of the May 28 reconvergence horizon’s comparator drop — the dashboard is simpler, but a falsifying event requires a heavier operator response.

The published reversal procedure is as follows. A fresh IRGC seizure cluster, a rupture indicator, or a comprehensive deal signal that contradicts the all-into-stalemate framing triggers the reversal. The methodology page would be updated to re-introduce Rule C alongside Rule B, with rule-posterior weights re-anchored against the post-event evidence rather than the pre-horizon read-grid. The published headline P(reopen) would move under the reversal in a single discrete step: a fresh IRGC cluster carries a calibrated −3 to −5 pp impact, taking P(reopen) from ~6.3% toward ~1–3% in the rupture-elevated scenario, with P(rupture-scenario) re-anchoring from 23% toward 28%–35%. The watch-list re-tags the IRGC Naval channel back to a corroboration indicator under Rule C, with the corroborating event already in the published record. Tracker A conditional liveness re-anchors to the new P(rupture-scenario) value.

None of this fires today — the channel was silent through the full calendar day. But the procedure’s existence and its substantive cost mean that the dashboard’s residual upside-detection bandwidth is not symmetric in the two directions: a positive-side comprehensive-deal signal lands inside the existing Rule B framing with a calibrated +3 to +5 pp impact handled by routine update; a negative-side IRGC cluster forces a methodology-page reversal with operator discretion required at the rule-weight re-anchoring step. This asymmetry was a known cost of the comparator drop and is published on the methodology page.
