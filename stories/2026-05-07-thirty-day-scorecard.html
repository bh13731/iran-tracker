The 30-Day Scorecard: What Each Side Has Actually Won in the First Month of Ceasefire

May 7, 2026 — Day 30 Analysis Cease-fire Hormuz
Day 30. The ceasefire completes its first full calendar month today. P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, unchanged. The probability has not moved in nine consecutive days. This is the moment to close the books on what one month of pause has actually produced — not as diplomatic aspiration, but as measurable military and political output. The scorecard is asymmetric: Iran recovered launchers and made no concessions; the US held the blockade and produced no diplomatic opening. The second month inherits a stalemate that the first month made structurally harder to break.

The Ceasefire as a Measurement Problem

The conventional frame for ceasefire assessment is diplomatic: has any deal moved forward? The answer here is unambiguous. Islamabad Round 1 produced a 2-week truce. Round 2 was postponed indefinitely on Day 15. No Round 3 date has been announced. The comprehensive peace deal that would unlock US blockade removal has no negotiating track. On diplomatic output, Month 1 produced approximately zero.

But the ceasefire is not only a diplomatic instrument. It is also a military pause with asymmetric effects. While no deal emerged, both sides used the pause to change their positions on the underlying military equation. Those changes are measurable — and they determine the strategic starting position for Month 2.

Launcher recovery. The single most consequential military development of Month 1 is Iran’s launcher reconstitution. US officials told the NYT on April 19 that Iran had spent the ceasefire digging approximately 100 launchers out of hardened underground storage facilities. Combined with surviving operational launchers (~102 as of April 8), the effective total reached roughly 200. This doubled Iran’s daily potential ballistic missile launch capacity from ~8–18 to potentially ~16–36 per day across all theaters. If the ceasefire ends tomorrow, Iran returns to the conflict with meaningfully greater ballistic capacity than when the truce began.

BM stockpile intact. Iran’s missile inventories have not changed. US officials assessed ~70% of the pre-war ballistic missile stockpile retained as of April 19. Thirty days later, that figure is unchanged — no missiles have been expended, no stocks depleted. Iran loses nothing from the pause on the stockpile side.

Proxy channel maintained. Direct Iran fire has been silent for 26 days. Iran-aligned militia proxy operations have not stopped. Kuwait was struck by Iraq-launched drones on April 24 (Day 17 of fire-silence). The proxy infrastructure — the distribution network of Hezbollah, Kata’ib Hezbollah, and Iraqi Shia militia that Iran uses to impose costs below the direct-fire threshold — remained operational throughout Month 1. Iran kept pressure on below the ceasefire’s effective coverage.

IRGC Navy demonstrated capability. The April 22 ship seizures established that Iran’s naval instrument is functional and escalation-ready. The Navy is the least-degraded element of Iran’s military. Demonstrating its willingness and capability to seize commercial vessels — without triggering kinetic US retaliation — is an intelligence and deterrence output, regardless of how the ships are eventually resolved.

Cost to Iran: none that can be measured. Economically, Iran continues to suffer from the US blockade and depressed oil revenues. But no new costs were imposed in Month 1 that were not already running at ceasefire start.

Interceptor recovery. The most significant US gain in Month 1. JINSA reported Gulf Patriot stocks at roughly 25% on Day 40 of the conflict. Twenty-six days of fire-silence later, that figure is recovering. Unlike Iran’s launcher reconstitution (a known ISW-confirmed figure), Patriot resupply volume is not publicly reported, but the strategic logic is clear: each day of Iranian direct-fire silence is a day of coalition interceptor resupply. The coalition’s binding constraint in any resumed conflict was interceptor availability, not launcher superiority. Month 1 improved their position on that constraint.

Blockade held. The US maintained its position that the blockade would not lift absent a comprehensive peace deal. No partial arrangement or memorandum was accepted. Iran made no progress on what it most needs: Hormuz access for oil exports. The blockade representing the US’s primary coercive instrument remained intact through Day 30.

Carrier posture maximized. Three carrier strike groups in theater is the maximum achievable US naval presence. It is simultaneously the strongest signal of commitment and the most expensive forward posture. The third carrier was ordered after the April 22 ship seizures, signaling US resolve not to back down from Iranian naval coercion.

Cost to the US: operational expense and strategic rigidity. 50,000 US personnel in theater for 30 days at full combat readiness is a significant operational cost. More importantly, three-carrier posture may now be structurally locking in the stalemate: Iran cannot accept any deal visible domestically as capitulation under three-carrier pressure, which means the very posture designed to force resolution may be preventing it. The US has achieved maximum coercion, but coercion without a negotiating channel produces no output.

The Asymmetry in Month-1 Outcomes

A scorecard is only meaningful against objectives. Both sides entered the ceasefire with different goals:

Iran’s stated objectives entering Month 1: Survive the pause without capitulating on Hormuz, recover military capacity, keep the proxy channel open, avoid a comprehensive deal that requires visible nuclear or missile concessions.

US objectives entering Month 1: Use the blockade as leverage to force a comprehensive deal, deter IRGC naval escalation, give the diplomatic track (Islamabad) enough space to produce results.

Against those objectives: Iran met all of its Month-1 objectives. The US met its deterrence objective (no new IRGC direct fire) but failed on diplomatic output. The blockade is intact but has produced no movement toward the comprehensive deal it was designed to extract.

This asymmetry is structural, not accidental. The ceasefire’s benefit to Iran (military recovery time, no forced concessions) is automatic — it requires only that Iran not fire directly, which costs nothing given its degraded aerospace force. The ceasefire’s benefit to the US (diplomatic progress) is contingent on Iranian cooperation that Iran has no current incentive to provide.

What Month 2 Inherits

The 55% stalemate scenario sits unchanged. The 23% rupture scenario has not been triggered. The two scenarios that would move P(reopen) — partial arrangement (15%) and comprehensive deal (7%) — both require a diplomatic track that does not exist.

Month 2 begins with Iran in a structurally stronger military position than Month 1 started: ~200 effective launchers vs ~102, without any diplomatic concession extracted in exchange. The US begins Month 2 with a stronger interceptor posture but no new diplomatic opening. The two-ships leverage trap (analyzed May 6) remains unresolved. China has not engaged on Hormuz despite 30 days of direct economic incentive to do so.

Three structural dynamics characterize the Month 2 starting position:

The negotiating calendar is compressed. If any diplomatic progress is to occur within the Hormuz 30-day rolling window (currently May 7 – June 6), a Round 3 date must be announced in the next few days to allow minimum viable negotiation time. The watch-list credit for a Round 3 date (+5–10 pp) is load-bearing for any probability upside in the current window. With each day of silence, that window for execution narrows.

Iran’s strategic patience has a demonstrated floor. Thirty days of ceasefire with zero diplomatic concession and no rupture establishes that Iran can sustain the stalemate at very low cost. The 55% stalemate scenario increasingly reflects not a passive equilibrium but a revealed strategic preference. Month 2 will test whether that patience holds under the same conditions or whether new pressures (economic, domestic, or proxy-related) shift the equation.

The rupture tail is the dominant downside. With no positive path active, the 23% rupture scenario is the highest-probability non-stalemate outcome. Rupture would collapse P(reopen) by 13–15 pp immediately. The specific rupture tripwires (proxy attack on US personnel, IRGC naval escalation resumption, Iranian domestic fracture) have not been triggered but remain live. Month 2 is when sustained stalemate begins generating its own rupture pressure through accumulated frustration, economic pain, and domestic political cycles.

30-Day verdict: Iran won Month 1 on military metrics (launchers: ~doubled; concessions: zero) while the US won on deterrence metrics (direct fire: zero; blockade: intact). Neither has moved the diplomatic needle. The scorecard does not favor a deal in Month 2: Iran has less reason to concede now than at Day 1; the US has more carrier presence but the same negotiating posture. P(Hormuz reopen 30d) = ~15% is the same number that started the week, the same number it was at Day 21, and it may be the same number at Day 60 unless one of the low-probability positive scenarios activates.

Bottom Line

P(Hormuz reopens within 30 days) = ~15%, unchanged. The first month of ceasefire was more productive for Iran than for the US on the metrics that will determine Month 2’s outcome. Iran enters Month 2 with roughly twice the effective launcher count, no forced concessions, and a still-functional proxy channel. The US enters Month 2 with more interceptors and a third carrier, but no diplomatic opening and a coercive posture that may now be structurally preventing the deal it was designed to force. The 30-day window continues to roll forward. What it needs — a Round 3 date, a Chinese engagement signal, or a carrier de-escalation gesture — is the same short list it has needed since Day 20. That list has not changed. Neither has the probability.
