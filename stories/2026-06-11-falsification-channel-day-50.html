The One Channel That Could Turn the Whole Dashboard

Most of the dashboard’s recent landmarks — calibration crossings, anchor-staleness flips, fire-silence round numbers — are explicitly insulated from the dial by design. The IRGC Naval channel is different in kind. Under the single-rule reversal procedure adopted at the May 28 reconvergence horizon, the dashboard publishes only Rule B (all-into-stalemate), and a Rule B-falsifying event does not produce an automatic in-rule update. The one event that would force a heavier response — a separately-published methodology-page reversal re-introducing Rule C as primary — is a fresh IRGC seizure cluster. That makes the naval channel the dashboard’s load-bearing tripwire: continued silence corroborates the published architecture; a single new seizure would partially dismantle it.

So the direction of the evidence matters. Fifty compounded days with no fresh seizure are not neutral — they are fifty days of accumulating evidence that the all-into-stalemate read is the right one. The channel is doing exactly what the methodology asks of a falsification indicator: it has had every opportunity to fire and has not. The longer it stays quiet, the more the April 22 event looks like a closed, one-off act rather than the opening move of a campaign.

The double-negative read. The April 22 seizure sits in a peculiar equilibrium: the vessels are held but not released (Iran has not cashed the leverage into any diplomatic concession) and held but not escalated (no second seizure, no naval incident, no widening). Both negatives have now run fifty days. That pattern is the signature of a single-event leverage trap — an instrument Iran cannot release without looking like capitulation and cannot exploit without a deal to trade it into — rather than the signature of an escalation-pattern opening salvo, which would have produced follow-on incidents inside the first few weeks.
