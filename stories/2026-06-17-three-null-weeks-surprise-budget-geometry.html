Reading the Calibration Channel by the Week

Every day the post-horizon regime publishes a single calibration number: the joint probability, under Rule B’s implied per-day null rate p ≈ 0.93, of seeing the observed run of all-null days. Each null day multiplies it by 0.93, so it falls a little under three percentage points per day at current depth. Read daily, that descent looks irregular — sometimes it crosses a round number, usually it doesn’t. Read by the week, it resolves into something exact.

The reason is that the regime has a natural seven-day rhythm built into it. The reconvergence horizon fired on a calendar date (May 28), and the methodology counts weekly cycles from there. Each completed cycle is exactly seven null days, so each cycle multiplies the calibration baseline by the same constant: 0.937 ≈ 0.60. Three cycles have now closed, and their cycle-close reads sit on that geometric ladder.

The Geometric Law

Sixty, thirty-seven, twenty-two. Each cycle-close read is roughly 0.60 times the one before it — not by coincidence but by construction, because seven null days is seven factors of 0.93, and 0.937 is the per-cycle multiplier. The weekly frame strips the day-to-day noise and exposes the constant the daily 0.93 hides.

The third cycle is the first to clear a qualitative bar: it opened at 0.9315 ≈ 34% and never rose above 40% across its seven days — the first weekly cycle the regime has published wholly inside the asymmetric surprise budget. Week 1 lived entirely above 50%; week 2 straddled the 50% line (it crossed sub-50% on June 6, mid-cycle); week 3 is the first cycle for which every published day was a day on which a further null was, by the regime’s own calibration, less likely than not.

What the multiplier means. A 0.60 per-week factor is a half-life of roughly ten days for the surprise budget: every ~10 null days the calibration baseline halves. That is a fast decay — fast enough that a single quiet fortnight carries the regime from “modal” (above 50%) into “mildly improbable” (sub-30%), and a quiet month would carry it into single digits. The decay being geometric rather than linear is the whole point: it is the signature of independent daily draws at a fixed rate, which is exactly the model the calibration baseline is built on.

Why a Cleaner Decay Still Can’t Re-Estimate the Rate

It is tempting to read the third clean ratio as confirmation that the model is working — or, conversely, as mounting proof that the 0.93 rate was set too high. The June 13 and June 15 stories established the correct, narrower reading, and the weekly frame does not change it; it only sharpens the temptation.

The geometric decay is precisely what you would observe if p = 0.93 is correct and this ceasefire happens to be sitting deep in the left tail of the distribution. It is also precisely what you would observe if the true rate is higher — say 0.97 — and the budget is simply decaying more slowly than 0.93 implies while still tracing a clean geometric curve at its own (gentler) multiplier. A perfectly geometric three-cycle decay is consistent with a wide band of underlying rates, because a single uninterrupted ceasefire is one realization of the process. The streak length and the parameter p are confounded, and no amount of staring at one streak — daily or weekly — separates a true rate from a tail draw of a lower one.

This is the same identification structure the June 12 plateau-vs-staleness flat line surfaced on the depletion side: a real, honestly-descending number that cannot, alone, distinguish a mis-specified model from a deep draw of a correct one. Re-estimating p requires an independent handle on the rate — a far longer run (which no single ceasefire supplies inside a decision-relevant window), a cross-sectional reference class of comparable ceasefire-architecture episodes, or an actual watch-list event. And the watch-list event is the self-erasing case: the moment one fires, it re-inflates the implied rate and resets the surprise budget, so the only evidence that could re-estimate p downward in confidence is the same evidence that ends the streak the re-estimation was about.

Why none of this touches the dial. The calibration baseline is decoupled from the scenario ladder by design. Even if the regime did re-estimate the null rate upward — concluding the post-horizon stalemate is stickier than the May-28 calibration assumed — the effect on P(reopen) would be negligibly bearish: a stickier stalemate adds weight to the 70% Stalemate scenario (P(open)=0, contributes nothing) and removes only a sliver from the thin 7% Comprehensive channel, a sub-point drift swamped by the published ±8 pp band. The surprise budget can fall through the single digits without the ladder owing it a move.
